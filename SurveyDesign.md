**Research title and rationale**
The research topic proposed is 'AI and Machine Learning in Cybersecurity Threat Detection: Adoption, Perceived Effectiveness and Practitioner Trust'. Machine learning can be used to process network traffic, system logs, and other security data to detect suspicious patterns or behavior. Sarker et al. (2020) discuss how data-driven models can help in intrusion detection and security decisions; Shaukat et al. (2020) demonstrate the use of machine-learning techniques in a variety of cybersecurity tasks. The survey is based on the perceptions of practitioners, since use of a technical system is not necessarily a sign of trust, integration, and use of an AI-enabled detection system in organisations. According to NIST (2023), the characteristics of trustworthy AI are related to: validity, reliability, transparency, explainability, privacy, security and resilience.



**1 Objectives of the survey**
The main objective is to explore the application and evaluation of AI and machine learning in threat detection by cybersecurity and IT security professionals. The survey will:s
• assess how much the organisation is currently using AI or machine-learning threat detection tools;
• assess perceived effects on detection speed, known-threat accuracy, novel-threat detection, false-positive reduction and analyst workload;
• assess practitioner confidence in taking action on alerts generated by AI and determine the primary obstacle to adopting AI; and
• glean a concrete suggestion to enhance the efficiency and reliability of AI-powered threat identification.
These objectives support the wider project by linking technology adoption with operational outcomes and practitioner trust. They also reflect the NIST Cybersecurity Framework's emphasis on continuous, context-specific management of cybersecurity risk (NIST, 2024).

**2 Survey conduct and distribution**
The group will create the questionnaire in Google Forms and test it with 3-5 eligible volunteers prior to releasing. Clarity, response options, completion time and technical operation will be assessed by the pilot participants. The initial responses will not be part of their final data set. It will take about 5 minutes to complete and will be open for 2 weeks.
A standard recruitment message will be sent to LinkedIn security groups, security professionals and university networks, including individuals with security-related roles. One neutral reminder may be sent by the group after 7 days. The invitation will not be displayed in a manner that reveals who has taken part and managers will not be requested to identify or pressure employees. The survey link and instructions will be the same for everyone to ensure consistent administration.

**3 Selected sample**
The target population are adults (18 years or older) with at least 6 months of experience in cybersecurity, security operations, network administration, systems administration, IT risk, incident response, or other threat detection-related position. The goal is to get 100 responses completed. Purposive sampling is suitable as the information answers need to be relevant to the professional knowledge. If there is a limited number of snowballs, the link can be shared with other eligible practitioners by asking the eligible participants.
The group will track who is participating and by job role and experience level and try to get more people involved if one group is over-represented. This is a non-probability method that may be suitable for a class project, but can result in self-selection and network bias. As such, the findings will represent the practitioners participating in the study and not be statistically representative of all cybersecurity practitioners.

**4 Structure of the survey**
The information page will include the following: 'Clicking on I consent and wish to continue' indicates consent. Clicking on 'I do not consent' will close this form. The consent screen does not count towards the 8 substantive survey questions. Questions 1-7 are quantitative, close-ended items and Question 8 is an open-ended qualitative item.
The proposed questionnaire consists of eight questions. Question 1 asks participants, “What is your current position best described as?” The available response options are: Security/SOC analyst; Security engineer; Network or systems administrator; Cybersecurity manager or consultant; IT risk or governance professional; and Other IT role with security responsibilities.

Question 2 asks, “How much experience do you have in cybersecurity or IT security?” Participants can select from the following options: 6–11 months; 1–3 years; 4–6 years; 7–10 years; or More than 10 years.

Question 3 asks, “How much are you using AI or machine learning in your organisation for cybersecurity threat detection?” The response options are: Not used; Being considered; Pilot or trial use; Limited operational use; Regular use across multiple security processes; or Unsure.

Question 4 asks, “How effective have you found AI or machine-learning tools to be in each area, based on your experience?” This question uses a five-point matrix with the response options: Very ineffective; Ineffective; Neither effective nor ineffective; Effective; Very effective; and Not applicable. Participants will rate AI or machine-learning tools in relation to detection speed, known-threat accuracy, detection of unusual or previously unseen behaviour, reduction of false positives, and reduction of analyst workload.

Question 5 asks, “What is your level of confidence in using an alert created by AI to assist with an investigation or response decision?” The available responses are: Not at all confident; Slightly confident; Moderately confident; Very confident; Completely confident; or No direct experience.

Question 6 asks, “What is the biggest challenge in your organisation to effective threat detection with AI or machine learning?” Participants can choose from: Poor or insufficient data; False positives; Limited explainability; Integration with existing systems; Skills shortage; Cost or resources; Privacy or security concerns; No major barrier; or Unsure.

Question 7 asks, “In the next two years, will your organisation use AI or machine learning more for threat detection?” The response options are: Very unlikely; Unlikely; Neither likely nor unlikely; Likely; Very likely; or Unsure.

Finally, Question 8 asks, “What is the most effective change that would enhance the effectiveness and trustworthiness of AI/machine learning threat detection in your organisation?” This is an open-text response question. Participants will be reminded not to name their organisation, individuals, systems, vulnerabilities or security incidents.

**5 Data analysis plans**
The group will export answers to a spreadsheet and check for duplicate, ineligible or substantially incomplete answers. Closed ended answers will be summarised in terms of counts and percentages. Experience and adoption will be cross-tabulated and each effectiveness row and the confidence item will be reported using the median, distribution of responses and an appropriate chart. The group can make comparisons across general experience groups, but should refrain from making inferences if the numbers of subgroups are too small. The items ‘Unsure' and ‘not applicable' and those that are not responded to will be reported separately and will not be treated as negative ratings (Creswell & Creswell, 2023).
The group will de-identify the information in Question 8, read all the answers, assign initial codes and then merge codes together to form themes, e.g., data quality, explainability, integration, human oversight or skills. Themes will be considered in relation to the original responses and reported with counts and short de-identified examples as appropriate. The structured thematic process is based on the approach of Braun and Clarke (2021). There will be at least two group members reviewing the coding framework to ensure that interpretations are discussed and documented.

**6 Ethical considerations**
The survey will follow the current National Statement on Ethical Conduct in Human Research (NHMRC et al., 2025). Participation will be voluntary and will be restricted to adults who are eligible. Participants will be informed, prior to consent, about the study, the estimated amount of time, who will use the data, any possible discomfort, who the researcher is, and the opportunity to withdraw before submitting. There is no payment offered and pressure from the employers or lecturers during recruitment through professional networks will be avoided.
No names, email addresses, employer names, IP address, and exact system and incident details will be requested in the form. The participants will be asked not to reveal operational information or any other confidential vulnerabilities in the open-text response. Data will be transferred to a password-protected storage space on the university and will only be accessible to the research group, and will be deleted from the survey platform after downloading. Results will be reported overall. Data will be deleted securely once the assessment/review requirements have been met and will not be stored after that. Since a response is anonymous, the information page will include an explanation that a response submitted cannot be normally withdrawn once it has been separated from identifying information. Data collection will only commence when the lecturer or institutional process has approved the survey.
